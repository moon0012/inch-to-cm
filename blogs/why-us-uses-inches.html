Why Does the US Still Use Inches? The Surprising History

The United States is one of only three countries that haven't officially adopted the metric system. Here's the fascinating story of how America ended up measuring things differently from the rest of the world.

Published on April 8, 2026 · 8 min read

A Nation Built on Inches

Walk into any hardware store in the United States, and you'll find tape measures marked in inches, lumber sold in feet, and nails sized by the penny. Drive down the highway, and distances are posted in miles. Step on a scale, and your weight shows up in pounds. For over 300 million Americans, the imperial system isn't just a measurement standard — it's woven into the fabric of daily life.

But here's the thing: the US stands nearly alone in this. Out of 195 countries in the world, only the United States, Myanmar, and Liberia have not officially adopted the metric system as their primary standard. So what happened? Why did the rest of the world move on while America held firm to its inches, feet, and miles?

The answer involves pirates, Thomas Jefferson, a forgotten act of Congress, and billions of dollars in sunk costs.

The Colonial Origins

When British colonists arrived in North America, they brought their measurement system with them. The English system of inches, feet, yards, and miles had been evolving for centuries, rooted in practical human-scale references. An inch was roughly the width of a man's thumb. A foot was, well, the length of a foot. A yard was the distance from King Henry I's nose to his outstretched thumb.

These measurements were imprecise by modern standards, but they worked well enough for farming, construction, and trade in the colonies. By the time the United States declared independence in 1776, the imperial system was deeply embedded in American law, commerce, and culture.

Jefferson's Metric Curiosity

Interestingly, Thomas Jefferson was an early advocate for a decimal-based measurement system. In 1790, he proposed a system remarkably similar to what would become the metric system. But Congress was slow to act, and when France formally introduced the meter in 1799, the US was already dealing with the political turmoil of its early years.

There's even a popular story — partly true — that a French scientist named Joseph Dombey was sailing to the US in 1793 carrying a copper kilogram and a copper meter to present to Congress. His ship was blown off course to the Caribbean, where he was captured by pirates and died in captivity. The metric standards never arrived. While this alone didn't determine America's measurement future, it makes for a compelling "what if."

The World Goes Metric (Without America)

Throughout the 19th and 20th centuries, country after country adopted the metric system. The Treaty of the Metre in 1875, signed by 17 nations including the United States, established international metric standards. But signing the treaty and actually converting were two very different things.

Britain began its slow transition in the 1960s. Australia switched in the 1970s. Canada followed suit. Even countries with strong ties to the British imperial system made the leap. But the US kept finding reasons to wait.

The Metric Conversion Act of 1975

The closest America came to going metric was in 1975, when Congress passed the Metric Conversion Act. President Gerald Ford signed it into law, and a Metric Board was established to coordinate the transition. There was just one problem: the conversion was entirely voluntary.

Without mandatory requirements, industries that had already invested billions in imperial-based tooling, manufacturing equipment, and training had little incentive to switch. The Metric Board had no enforcement power and could only "coordinate" and "plan." By 1982, President Reagan disbanded the board, and America's metric moment had passed.

The Cost of Not Converting

In 1999, NASA lost the $125 million Mars Climate Orbiter because one engineering team used metric units while another used imperial. The spacecraft approached Mars at the wrong angle and was destroyed. It remains one of the most expensive unit conversion errors in history.

Why Switching Is So Hard

The reasons the US hasn't fully adopted the metric system are practical, economic, and cultural.

The Economic Argument

Converting an entire nation's infrastructure is staggeringly expensive. Every road sign would need replacement (there are over 4 million miles of roads in the US). Every machine tool, every architectural plan, every plumbing fitting is built to imperial specifications. The National Institute of Standards and Technology has estimated that a full conversion would cost hundreds of billions of dollars over several decades.

For comparison, when the UK converted its road signs to metric, the project was estimated at over 700 million pounds — and the UK is far smaller than the US. In fact, Britain never actually completed that conversion; British roads still use miles.

The Cultural Attachment

Americans intuitively understand imperial measurements in a way that metric doesn't quite replicate for them. Ask an American how tall they are, and they'll say "five foot ten," not "178 centimeters." A comfortable room temperature is "72 degrees," not "22 Celsius." These reference points are learned from childhood and reinforced daily.

There's also an element of national identity. Rightly or wrongly, some Americans view the imperial system as a form of cultural independence — doing things "the American way." Polls consistently show that a majority of Americans oppose mandatory metrication.

The "Good Enough" Factor

In practice, the US already uses metric where it matters most. Science, medicine, and the military all operate in metric. International trade uses metric measurements. Nutrition labels show grams and milligrams. The 2-liter soda bottle is perhaps the most familiar metric measurement in American life. For everything else, the existing system works well enough that the pain of switching outweighs the benefits.

The UK's Half-Metric Compromise

It's worth noting that the United Kingdom — the birthplace of the imperial system — hasn't fully converted either. The UK officially went metric in 1965, but decades later, Brits still measure their weight in stone, their height in feet and inches, and their driving distances in miles. Beer is sold in pints. Road signs show miles per hour.

This hybrid approach shows that metrication isn't an all-or-nothing proposition. In practice, most countries that "went metric" still retain some imperial measurements in everyday speech and cultural contexts. The difference is that their official systems, education, and commerce operate in metric.

What Does This Mean for Everyday Life?

For Americans, the practical impact is constant unit conversion. If you're buying furniture from a European retailer, the dimensions are in centimeters. If you're following a recipe from a British cookbook, you might encounter grams instead of cups. If you're working on a car with both American and imported parts, you'll need two sets of wrenches.

Will the US Ever Switch?

Probably not in any dramatic, overnight way. The trend in the US has been gradual, sector-by-sector adoption of metric where it makes economic sense. The pharmaceutical industry already uses metric. So does the auto industry (most modern cars use metric fasteners). Scientific research is exclusively metric.

What's more likely is a continued drift toward a dual-system reality, similar to what the UK has experienced. Younger generations, exposed to metric through science education and global media, may become more comfortable with both systems. International commerce and digital tools will continue to bridge the gap.

In the meantime, the inch isn't going anywhere. It's been part of American life for over 400 years, and it has a staying power that no act of Congress has been able to dislodge. Whether you think that's a charming quirk or a frustrating inconvenience probably depends on which side of the Atlantic you call home.
