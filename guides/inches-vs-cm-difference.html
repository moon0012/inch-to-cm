Inches vs Centimeters: What's the Difference? Complete Comparison

Published April 8, 2026 · 7 min read

Two Systems, One Purpose

Inches and centimeters both measure length, but they belong to entirely different measurement systems. Inches are part of the Imperial system (also called the US customary system), while centimeters belong to the metric system. The relationship between them is fixed: one inch equals exactly 2.54 centimeters. But the story behind how we ended up with two competing systems is worth understanding, because it affects everything from the clothes you buy to the medicine your doctor prescribes.

A Brief History of the Inch

The inch has roots stretching back to at least the 7th century. The word "inch" comes from the Latin "uncia," meaning one-twelfth -- because an inch was originally defined as one-twelfth of a foot. Early definitions were wonderfully inconsistent. In medieval England, an inch was sometimes described as the length of three barleycorns placed end to end. King Edward II formalized this in the 14th century, which at least gave everyone in England a common standard.

The problem, of course, was that barleycorns vary in size. Over the centuries, the inch was refined and standardized. By the 20th century, different countries using the inch had slightly different definitions. The British inch and the American inch were not quite the same length, which caused real problems in manufacturing and trade.

In 1959, six countries (the United States, United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, and South Africa) signed an agreement defining the international inch as exactly 25.4 millimeters, or 2.54 centimeters. This is the definition used worldwide today.

A Brief History of the Centimeter

The centimeter is a much younger unit. It was born from the French Revolution -- not just a political revolution but an intellectual one. In 1791, the French Academy of Sciences proposed a new measurement system based on nature rather than the human body or royal decree. They defined the meter as one ten-millionth of the distance from the North Pole to the Equator along a meridian passing through Paris.

The centimeter is simply one-hundredth of a meter. "Centi" comes from the Latin for "hundred." The beauty of the metric system lies in its decimal structure: everything scales by factors of 10. One meter is 100 centimeters. One centimeter is 10 millimeters. No memorizing that 12 inches make a foot or that 3 feet make a yard.

Over the following two centuries, nearly every country on Earth adopted the metric system. Today, only three countries have not officially adopted it as their primary measurement system: the United States, Liberia, and Myanmar.

Where Each Unit Is Used Geographically

Countries That Primarily Use Inches

The United States is the largest country that still uses inches as its primary unit for everyday measurements. Americans measure height in feet and inches, buy lumber in inch dimensions, and set their thermostats in Fahrenheit. The UK uses a mixed approach: road signs show miles, people often state their height in feet and inches, but official documents and scientific work use metric units. Canada is similar -- metric is official, but inches persist in construction, real estate, and casual conversation.

Countries That Use Centimeters

Virtually everywhere else. The European Union, all of Asia, Africa, South America, and Oceania use the metric system. Japan, China, India, Germany, Brazil, Australia -- all measure in centimeters and meters for everyday purposes. When you shop online from a seller in any of these countries, product dimensions will be listed in centimeters.

Pros and Cons of Each System

Advantages of Inches

  • Fractional precision. The system of halves, quarters, eighths, and sixteenths is well-suited for practical trades like woodworking and construction, where physically dividing a piece in half repeatedly is intuitive.
  • Human-scale units. A foot is roughly the length of an adult foot. An inch is about the width of a thumb. These relationships make rough estimation natural.
  • Entrenched infrastructure. In the US, everything from building codes to pipe fittings to screw threads is built around imperial measurements. Switching would require enormous investment.

Disadvantages of Inches

  • Complex conversions. 12 inches in a foot, 3 feet in a yard, 1,760 yards in a mile. These relationships are not intuitive and require memorization.
  • Fraction arithmetic. Adding 3/8 and 5/16 is harder than adding 0.375 and 0.3125. Working with fractions slows down calculations.
  • International incompatibility. Using inches puts American manufacturers at a disadvantage in global markets, and forces consumers to convert when buying international products.

Advantages of Centimeters

  • Decimal simplicity. Converting between millimeters, centimeters, meters, and kilometers is just a matter of moving the decimal point.
  • Global standard. Scientific papers, international trade agreements, and manufacturing specifications all use metric units.
  • Consistent with other SI units. The metric system ties length, mass, and volume together logically. One liter is 1,000 cubic centimeters. One milliliter of water weighs one gram.

Disadvantages of Centimeters

  • Less intuitive subdivisions for some tasks. Splitting a board into thirds or sixths does not produce clean decimal numbers.
  • Transition costs. For countries deeply invested in imperial infrastructure, switching is expensive and disruptive.

Why Both Systems Still Exist

If the metric system is simpler and nearly universal, why hasn't the whole world switched? The answer is mostly economic and cultural. The United States attempted metrication in the 1970s with the Metric Conversion Act of 1975, but it was voluntary, and without legal teeth, the effort stalled. The cost of replacing signs, tools, building materials, and retraining an entire workforce was seen as too high relative to the benefit.

There is also cultural attachment. People relate to measurements they grew up with. Telling an American that it is 32 degrees Celsius outside does not trigger the same instinctive understanding as saying it is 90 Fahrenheit. Similarly, saying someone is 183 centimeters tall does not land the same way as "six feet."

The result is a world where both systems coexist, and conversions are a daily necessity for anyone involved in international work, travel, or online shopping.

Real-World Consequences of Mixing Units

The Mars Climate Orbiter (1999): NASA lost a $125 million spacecraft because one team used imperial units (pound-force seconds) while another used metric units (newton-seconds) for thruster calculations. The probe entered the Martian atmosphere at the wrong angle and was destroyed.

The Gimli Glider (1983): An Air Canada Boeing 767 ran out of fuel mid-flight because ground crew calculated the fuel load in pounds instead of kilograms. The metric conversion error left the aircraft with less than half the fuel it needed. The pilots managed to glide to an emergency landing at a former airfield in Gimli, Manitoba.

These are extreme examples, but smaller mix-ups happen constantly -- furniture that does not fit, clothes ordered in the wrong size, building materials cut to the wrong length. Understanding both systems and converting between them accurately is a practical skill worth having.
